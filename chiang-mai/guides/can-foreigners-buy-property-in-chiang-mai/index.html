Can foreigners buy property in Chiang Mai? (2026)

Short answer: a foreigner can own a condominium unit in Chiang Mai outright (freehold), as long as the building still has foreign quota and the money comes from abroad. A foreigner cannot own land — which means you cannot simply buy a house with its plot in your own name. For houses, the legal routes are a registered lease of up to 30 years, owning the building while leasing the land, or a few narrower arrangements. Shortcuts that "get around" the land rule — mainly Thai nominee companies — are illegal and are being actively prosecuted.

The rest of this guide explains each option, what it costs, and the traps.

1. Land: the hard "no"

Thailand's Land Code, Section 86, restricts land ownership by foreigners; there is no general route for an individual foreigner to hold a land title deed (chanote, โฉนด) in their own name (Land Code translation, ThailandLawOnline). This applies in Chiang Mai exactly as in Bangkok or Phuket.

There is one narrow exception in Section 96 bis: a foreigner who brings in at least 40 million baht of qualifying investment may apply for permission to own up to one rai (1,600 m²) of residential land. It requires ministerial approval and is rarely used — it is not a realistic plan for most buyers (terms.law overview).

What about a Thai spouse? A Thai national can own land, including one married to a foreigner. In practice the Land Office usually asks the foreign spouse to sign a declaration that the money is the Thai spouse's own, so the land is the Thai spouse's property. Get a lawyer to explain what that means for you in divorce or inheritance before you fund the purchase.

2. Condos: the realistic freehold option

The Condominium Act B.E. 2522, Section 19 bis, lets foreigners together own up to 49% of the total sellable floor area of the units in a registered condominium building (Condominium Act translation; explainer). Three things follow:

  1. Quota is per building and measured by floor area, not unit count. Popular buildings in Nimman can be "full" for foreign freehold. Ask the building's juristic person (the condo management office) for a written confirmation of remaining foreign quota before you pay a deposit.
  2. Money must come from abroad. For most buyers (non-residents) the purchase money must be remitted into Thailand in foreign currency and documented by the receiving bank with a Foreign Exchange Transaction (FET) form — without it the Land Office will not register the unit under the foreign quota (ThaiLawOnline on the quota and FET). Baht you already hold in a Thai account usually does not count. Tell your bank the purpose ("purchase of condominium unit") when you send.
  3. Buying a unit that is already foreign-owned usually transfers that quota with it — but you still need your own FET evidence.

Status of the proposals you may have read about: the idea of raising the foreign condo quota from 49% to 75% has circulated since 2024 but, as of the sources we checked, has not been enacted — the 49% limit still applies (Bektu, June 2026; LAFS Legal). Don't buy on the assumption that it will pass.

Chiang Mai condo market, in numbers

  • Foreign buyers took 6,533 condo units across Thailand in H1 2026 (−8.8% year on year), worth ฿28.3 billion; China, Russia, Myanmar and the US were the top four buyer groups by value (REIC via The Nation, 11 Sep 2026).
  • In Chiang Mai, Chinese buyers have historically been the majority of foreign condo buyers (57.6% at one point) but their share has been falling while Myanmar, US and European buyers grow (REIC via The Nation).
  • Asking prices vary hugely by building and age. One 2026 listings analysis put the Chiang Mai median condo listing around ฿2.76 million (≈฿60,000/m²), with Nimman projects starting near ฿3 million (Own Property Abroad); another guide quotes Old City units at roughly ฿60,000–100,000/m² (Thaiger). These are listing prices, not transaction prices — use them only as a rough starting point and compare real recent sales in the same building.

3. Houses: leasehold, superficies, usufruct

If you want a house and garden — common for families in Hang Dong, San Sai or Mae Rim — the options are about rights over land, not ownership of it.

Registered lease (up to 30 years)

Under the Civil and Commercial Code, a lease of immovable property can run for at most 30 years (Section 540), and a lease longer than three years must be in writing and registered at the Land Office or it is enforceable only for three years (Section 538) (CCC translation). Always register.

The "30+30+30" myth. Many listings still advertise "60-year" or "90-year" leases built from pre-agreed renewals. In Supreme Court Decision No. 4655/2566 (decided 2023, widely reported from March 2025), Thailand's Supreme Court held that a pre-agreed renewal mechanism designed to exceed the statutory 30-year limit is unenforceable (case summary, ThailandLawOnline; Formichella & Sritawat; AustCham Thailand; Siam Legal). Value a lease as 30 years, full stop. A proposed 99-year lease law has also been discussed for years; it has not been enacted (Bektu).

Superficies (owning the building, not the land)

A superficies right (CCC Section 1410 onwards) lets you own a building on someone else's land. Combined with a registered land lease, it's a common way for a foreigner to own a house they build or buy. It must be registered.

Usufruct

A usufruct (CCC Section 1417 onwards) gives a right to use the property and take its income for a period or for life. It's often discussed for retirees or couples. It is not ownership and cannot simply be sold on.

Each of these has tax, inheritance and resale consequences — exactly the questions to bring to a lawyer.

4. The illegal shortcut: nominee companies

A common old sales pitch was: "set up a Thai company with Thai shareholders, the company buys the land, you control it." If the Thai shareholders are only fronts (nominees), that structure is illegal under the Foreign Business Act and the Land Code — the Land Code provides criminal penalties for acquiring land for a foreigner through a nominee, and the FBA penalises both the foreigner and the Thai who lends their name (FBA, BOI translation; terms.law on penalties). Thai authorities have been widening a nominee crackdown in 2025–2026, tracing ownership structures and Thai shareholders, and the Department of Lands sent Provincial Land Offices three urgent circulars in May 2026 on land held for foreigners through Thai proxies (ThaiLawOnline; Bangkok Post, "Business nominee crackdown widens"; Bangkok Post, "Nominee crackdown steps up").

If an agent or developer suggests a company structure to "own" land, walk away or get an independent Thai lawyer — one who is not recommended by the seller.

5. What it costs to transfer (rough guide)

At the Land Office, a sale typically involves (ThaiLawOnline glossary; PropertyScout):

Item Typical rate Usually paid by
Transfer fee 2% of the government appraised value Negotiable — often split 50/50
Specific Business Tax 3.3% (if the seller has owned < 5 years) — on the higher of sale price or appraised value Seller (by law), but check the contract
or Stamp duty 0.5% (only if SBT doesn't apply) Seller usually
Withholding tax Varies by seller type and years held Seller

New condos may add a sinking fund and up-front common-area fees. Leases have their own registration fee and stamp duty. Put who pays what in writing in the sale agreement.

6. A due-diligence checklist for Chiang Mai buyers

  • [ ] Title: for land/houses, what deed type is it (Chanote/โฉนด vs. weaker documents)? Ask your lawyer to search it at the Land Office.
  • [ ] Condo quota: written confirmation of remaining foreign quota from the juristic person.
  • [ ] FET plan: how the money will arrive, from which bank, with what stated purpose.
  • [ ] Debts: unpaid common fees on a resale unit (you need a debt-free letter for transfer).
  • [ ] Encumbrances: existing mortgage, lease or servitude registered on the title.
  • [ ] Off-plan risk: developer track record, escrow/payment schedule, completion penalties. Newer consumer-protection rules for off-plan deposits were introduced in 2025 — ask your lawyer which apply (terms.law).
  • [ ] Building permits for houses; zoning; access road (a land plot with no legal road access is a classic trap).
  • [ ] Lease vs. "ownership" wording: if it's a lease, the contract and the Land Office registration must say so. Treat "99-year" or "30+30+30" as 30.
  • [ ] Your own lawyer, independent of the seller and agent.
  • [ ] Agent: Thailand has no specific real-estate agent licence, so check an agency's company registration, years trading, office and written authority from the owner. (Spector can explain the market, but does not broker deals.)

7. Renting first is often the smart move

Many foreigners rent in Chiang Mai for 6–12 months before deciding where — or whether — to buy. Neighbourhoods feel very different across seasons (including the February–April burning season). See our guides on rent by neighbourhood and the renting checklist.
