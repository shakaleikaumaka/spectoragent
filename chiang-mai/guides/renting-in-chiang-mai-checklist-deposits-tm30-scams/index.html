Renting in Chiang Mai: the checklist (deposits, TM30, scams)

Most rentals in Chiang Mai go smoothly. The ones that don't usually fail in one of four places: paying the wrong person, an unclear deposit, a missing TM30, or a lease that says something different from what you were told. This checklist covers all four, in the order you'll meet them.

Step 1 — Before you pay anything: the three checks

Thai police and news reports describe one dominant rental-scam pattern: someone who isn't the owner (or has no right to let) advertises a real home with real photos, takes the deposit and first month's rent, and disappears. Viewing the home did not protect tenants in several documented cases — in some, the victims even signed a contract (Chiang Mai Property, "Rental Scams in Thailand", citing Thai police and news reports 2023–2026).

Three checks stop most of these:

  1. Who owns it? Ownership is shown by the title deed — chanote (โฉนดที่ดิน) for land/houses, the unit title (หนังสือกรรมสิทธิ์ห้องชุด, อ.ช.2) for a condo. Compare the name with the owner's ID and with the lease. The blue house book (ทะเบียนบ้าน) does not prove ownership. Anyone can ask the Land Office who the registered owner of a specific plot or unit is (bring a Thai speaker) (same source, citing the Department of Lands manual).
  2. Can this person let it? If an agent, relative or "manager" is letting for the owner, ask for a written authority signed by the owner, plus copies of the owner's ID and title. Note: Thailand has no specific licence for property agents, so an "agent card" proves nothing official — check the company's registration and track record instead. If you're renting from a tenant who sublets, ask for the head lease and the owner's written consent — under the Civil and Commercial Code a tenant can't sublet without it (s.544).
  3. Whose account? Pay into an account in the owner's (or the landlord company's) own name and read the payee name your banking app shows before you confirm. You can check an account against scam reports at checkgon.go.th (Royal Thai Police, in Thai) — a clean result only means no one has reported it yet.

Red flags: price far below similar units; "you must book today"; deposit requested before a viewing or before you've seen the title; an account in a third person's name; photos that show up in other listings (try a reverse image search).

If you've already paid a scammer: call 1441 (government anti-scam line, 24h) and your bank's fraud line immediately to freeze the receiving account, then make a police statement within 72 hours. Tourist Police 1155 helps in English (Chiang Mai Tourist Police: 053-248-130) (source; US Embassy, common scams). Anyone offering to "recover your money for a fee" is usually a second scam.

Step 2 — Deposits and move-in money

What's normal: one or two months' deposit plus the first month's rent in advance. On a ฿12,000 room, expect ฿24,000–36,000 on day one (Monthly Chiang Mai). Two months' deposit plus one month's rent is a common Thai arrangement — the amount alone is not a warning sign; who you pay and when is.

The 2025 rules for "business" landlords. Thailand treats residential leasing by business operators as a contract-controlled business. A new Contract Committee notification was published in the Royal Gazette on 6 June 2025 (Royal Gazette PDF, in Thai) and, per ThailandLawOnline's unofficial translation, applies to leases signed from 4 September 2025 where the landlord offers three or more residential units (ThailandLawOnline). Key points of that translation (verify with a lawyer):

Rule (covered landlords) What it says
Language & copies Lease in Thai, legible, two signed copies — you get one at signing
Deposit + advance cap Generally ≤ 3 months' rent combined for monthly-paid leases (≤ 1 year's rent for annually-paid long leases)
Deposit return Within 7 days if no damage; within 14 days with itemised, evidenced deductions
Utilities At the official supplier rate — no mark-up
Invoices In writing ≥ 3 days before the due date; you can ask to see the calculation
Move-in inspection Joint inspection and signed condition report (photos can be attached)
Early termination (tenant) After occupying ≥ 50% of the term, with 30 days' written notice
Banned clauses Forfeiting your deposit without your fault; charging for normal wear and tear; entering without notice (except emergencies); lock-outs or seizing belongings; unilateral rent/utility increases mid-term; automatic renewal without consent

Private landlords (one or two units) are generally outside these rules and governed by the Civil and Commercial Code — so your lease wording matters even more. Ask for the same protections in writing anyway; many good landlords already follow them.

Disputes with a real landlord (deposit not returned, surprise charges) go to the Office of the Consumer Protection Board (OCPB), hotline 1166, free (source).

Step 3 — The lease: read it before you sign

  • [ ] Names of landlord and tenant match their IDs/passports; the landlord matches the title deed (or there's a written authority).
  • [ ] Address and unit number exactly as on the title.
  • [ ] Term, rent, due date, payment method (account in landlord's name).
  • [ ] Deposit amount and when/how it's returned, and what can be deducted.
  • [ ] Electricity and water rates per unit, written down.
  • [ ] Who pays common fees, internet, repairs, pest control, air-con cleaning.
  • [ ] Early termination: notice period and what you lose.
  • [ ] Inventory and condition report with photos, signed by both.
  • [ ] Meter readings on move-in day, photographed.
  • [ ] TM30 clause — who files and by when (see Step 4).
  • [ ] Bilingual lease? If there's a Thai and an English version, know which one prevails.
  • [ ] Short-let warning: renting a condo by the day or week can be treated as running an unlicensed hotel — a separate offence from TM30 (Chiang Mai Property, TM30 guide). Monthly leases are the norm for a reason.

A lease of immovable property needs written evidence to be enforceable, and a lease longer than three years must be registered at the Land Office (Civil and Commercial Code s.538) — relevant if anyone offers you a multi-year deal.

Step 4 — TM30: the address notice you'll need later

What it is. TM30 tells Thai Immigration where a foreigner is staying. Under the Immigration Act (ss.37–38) the person who takes the foreigner in — for a rental, normally the owner/landlord (or their agent) — must file it within 24 hours of move-in. It's free, online at tm30.immigration.go.th or on paper at Chiang Mai Immigration (Investigation section, Airport Road) (Chiang Mai Property, TM30 guide; Chiang Mai Immigration).

Why you, the tenant, care. You'll need the TM30 receipt for things like a residence certificate (which banks and the land transport office ask for), visa extensions and 90-day reports. Ask for the PDF on move-in day and check: your name and passport number are right, the address matches the lease, and the "stay until" date covers your lease.

Who files in common situations (same source):

Situation Who files
You rent from a Thai owner The owner (or someone they assign)
Owner lets through an agency The agency, for the owner
Condo unit The unit's owner — not the building office (unless acting as the owner's messenger)
You sublet, or a partner/guest moves in with you You, for that person
You own and live in your condo You, for yourself

When a new TM30 is needed: a new address; a new person moving in; staying past the stated check-out date; or returning from abroad on a new visa-exempt entry or new single-entry visa. Returning on a re-entry permit or multiple-entry visa within the stated period generally does not need a new one. Trips within Thailand don't.

Fines: a landlord who doesn't notify can be fined up to ฿2,000 (hotels ฿2,000–10,000). The fine is the landlord's, but a missing TM30 holds up your paperwork. Chiang Mai Immigration does run sweeps — owners were fined in 2023, and a foreigner was fined in Saraphi in October 2026 (same source).

Changes coming? An immigration bill passed Thailand's House of Representatives 403–0 on 30 September 2026 and is before the Senate; the Cabinet draft kept the landlord's TM30 duty but would change other reporting rules (e.g. the 90-day report and your own change-of-address notice). Until it's published in the Royal Gazette, the current rules apply (same source, citing Prachatai and Thansettakij). Spector will update this section when the law changes.

Also remember (separate from TM30): if you stay in Thailand more than 90 days continuously, you file the 90-day report (TM47) — online, by post, or in person at Chiang Mai Immigration (Chiang Mai Immigration).

There is no official TM30 app. Any site or app charging you to "file your TM30" is a private service, not the Immigration Bureau.

Step 5 — Move-in day (15 minutes that save your deposit)

  • [ ] Photograph/video every room, including existing damage, with timestamps.
  • [ ] Photograph electricity and water meters.
  • [ ] Test air-con, hot water, Wi-Fi, locks, windows.
  • [ ] Sign the condition report; keep your copy.
  • [ ] Get the TM30 PDF (or the date the landlord will file it — today or within 24h).
  • [ ] Keep every payment slip.

Step 6 — Move-out

  • [ ] Give notice in writing, as the lease requires.
  • [ ] Joint inspection; compare with your move-in photos.
  • [ ] Final meter readings photographed.
  • [ ] Get the deposit return date (and, for covered landlords, the 7/14-day rule) in writing.
  • [ ] If there's a dispute: OCPB 1166.
